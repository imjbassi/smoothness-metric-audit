\begin{abstract}
Trajectory smoothness has been proposed as a cheap, training-free signal for
curating imitation-learning demonstrations, most recently by \rinse{}, which
introduces a Spectral Arc Length (SAL) variant and a contact-aware
Trajectory-Envelope Distance (TED). We audit those two metrics as published,
on robomimic data with real operator-skill and task-success labels. Three
results hold under every control we applied. First, \rinse{}'s SAL tracks
episode length. On can/mh it separates better from worse operators with
AUROC 0.952 while length alone achieves 0.957, the two agree within every
pair of operators, and inside a duration-matched band SAL's interval
includes chance (0.628 [0.378, 0.856]); square/mh behaves the same way
(0.898 against 0.936 for length, 0.480 in the matched band). Much of the
dependence is specific to \rinse{}'s unnormalized formula: the standard
SPARC definition correlates with length at 0.80 and 0.72 on the two tasks,
against 0.97 and 0.96. Second, on the two fixed-horizon machine-generated
splits, where length carries no information, \rinse{}'s SAL is weak against
task success (0.454 on can/mg, 0.643 on lift/mg) while standard SPARC and
raw mean jerk are strong (SPARC 0.871 and 0.891, jerk 0.706 and 0.870). TED
is below chance on can/mg (0.376) but above it on lift/mg (0.669), and
within individual can/mg policy checkpoints it is at chance (0.484), so its
inversion reflects which checkpoints it favors. Third, training GMM
behavior-cloning policies on can/mg (10 seeds per condition), curating by
TED costs a third of downstream success relative to a random subset of the
same size (0.240 vs.\ 0.360, $p{=}0.0001$; $0.001$ after Bonferroni
correction); the TED pool contains the fewest successes of any pool and the
most rollouts from checkpoints that never succeed. Against training on the
full pool (0.406), every metric we trained on is worse after correction,
TED by 0.166, while a pool curated by standard SPARC is better (0.480).
On can/mh, curating by SAL beats a random half (0.854 vs.\ 0.650) but
does no better than keeping the shortest episodes (0.878). One result from an
earlier version of this paper does not hold as stated: jerk's high AUROC
inside the duration-matched bands (0.896 on both tasks) comes from a single
pair of operators, and across the four better/worse operator pairs it ranges
(on the full sample) from 0.28 to 0.97 on Can and from 0.01 to 0.99 on
Square. A five-seed
diffusion-policy replication is reported as provisional. The claims concern
\rinse{}'s two formulas on three robomimic tasks, not smoothness metrics as
a class.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Behavior cloning inherits the quality of its demonstrations, and a growing
literature proposes to score and filter demonstrations automatically.
Training-free trajectory statistics are the cheapest family of such scores,
and the most developed recent proposal is \rinse{}~\citep{rinse}, which ranks
demonstrations by two smoothness metrics: Spectral Arc Length (SAL), a
frequency-domain measure adapted from rehabilitation science, and
Trajectory-Envelope Distance (TED), a contact-aware spatial deviation from a
B\'ezier-smoothed reference. \rinse{} reports that filtering by these metrics
matches or beats full-data training on robomimic tasks.

A recent line of audit work complicates this picture.
Bedi~\citep{bedi-audit} showed on a controlled testbed that action-only
curation scorers are blind or inverted on structural defects, and the
follow-up~\citep{bedi-policy} showed that five of seven curation metrics on a
LIBERO testbed achieved near-perfect detection AUROC by measuring episode
length rather than demonstration content, and that detection accuracy and
curation value are sharply decoupled. Those results, however, were obtained
with \emph{generic} metric implementations (a SPARC-style smoothness score
among them), with \emph{synthetically injected} defects, and with episode
length controlled by \emph{truncation}.

This paper asks the natural next question: do the specific, published metrics
of \rinse{} survive these controls on real data? Our audit differs from the
Bedi testbeds on three axes, each of which closes a gap that line of work
explicitly left open:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Named metrics, not proxies.} We implement SAL from its
published formula and TED from the \rinse{} appendix, including the
contact-boundary partition that is TED's distinguishing design feature. TED
has not previously been evaluated outside its authors' own
success-filtered setting.
\item \textbf{Real labels, not injected defects.} robomimic
can/mh~\citep{robomimic} carries \emph{human operator skill tiers}
(better/okay/worse) assigned to real teleoperators, and can/mg and lift/mg
carry real task-success outcomes of rollouts from checkpoints of SAC
training runs. No defect is synthetic.
\item \textbf{A length-free benchmark by construction.} In can/mg and
lift/mg every episode is exactly 150 steps. The episode-length confound documented
by~\citep{bedi-policy} cannot exist in this data, with no truncation
required. Truncation removes the confound by discarding signal; a
fixed-horizon dataset removes it without touching the trajectories.
\end{enumerate}

The scope of the evidence is worth stating before the results rather than
after them. The detection results, which involve no training, cover the two
robomimic tasks with operator skill tiers that we analyzed (Can and Square,
two operators per tier) and the two tasks with machine-generated splits (Can
and Lift). The downstream-training results cover Can only, under two policy
configurations: a GMM behavior-cloning policy, run on can/mg for all four
metrics, standard SPARC, and the full pool, and on can/mh for SAL and
episode length, all at ten seeds; and a diffusion policy, run on can/mg for
two of the four metrics at five seeds. The downstream claims we make about TED are therefore established
under behavior cloning only, and the diffusion results are provisional. We
make no claim about other tasks, about human teleoperation failures, about
smoothness metrics other than the ones named, or about other policy
configurations, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states what each
extension would require.

Our findings are narrower than an earlier version of this paper stated.
\rinse{}'s SAL ranks operator skill to the extent that skill is confounded
with how long the episode took: it matches the episode-length baseline
overall and within every pair of operators, and its advantage disappears
under normalization and duration matching. Much of that dependence comes
from \rinse{}'s formula, which omits the normalization and frequency cap of
the standard SPARC definition~\citep{sparc}. Against task success on
fixed-length data, \rinse{}'s SAL is weak on both machine-generated splits,
where standard SPARC and jerk are strong; TED is below chance on Can and
above it on Lift. Curating can/mg by TED produces a behavior-cloning policy
substantially worse than random subsampling of the same size, a result that
survives correction for multiple comparisons; the other metrics are not
distinguishable from random subsets after correction. Against training on
the full pool, all four metrics produce worse policies, while a pool
curated by standard SPARC produces a better one. On can/mh, where every
demonstration succeeds, curating by \rinse{}'s SAL improves on random
selection, but keeping the shortest episodes does at least as well. We withdraw one earlier claim, that
raw jerk becomes the strongest skill detector once duration is controlled:
that result is produced by a single pair of operators
(Section~\ref{sec:operators}).

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Trajectory-statistic curation.} \rinse{}~\citep{rinse} is the primary
subject of this audit; it assumes demonstration pools pre-filtered for task
success and validates on successful trajectories only, a scoping decision its
limitations section states explicitly. PSD~\citep{psd} scores demonstrations
by total spectral power of the end-effector trajectory and reports gains over
learning-based curation on robomimic-MH and a non-expert dataset. Neither
work evaluates on pools containing failures.

\textbf{Audits of curation metrics.} Bedi's testbed
papers~\citep{bedi-audit,bedi-policy,bedi-phase} established the
detection-vs-curation decoupling, documented the episode-length confound, and
reported a negative result on per-phase metric selection. We adopt their
framing and extend it to published metrics, real labels, and a natively
fixed-length benchmark. Where they control length by truncating to a fixed
prefix, our can/mg experiments require no such intervention.

\textbf{Evaluation practice.} Kress-Gazit et
al.~\citep{empirical-science} and RoboEval~\citep{roboeval} argue that binary
success rate under-describes policy quality, and~\citep{benchmarking-audit}
shows that widely used benchmarks admit shortcut solutions. Our results are a
data-side complement: the scores used to \emph{select training data} can also
be shortcut-driven, with episode duration as the shortcut.

\subsection{What each metric measures}
\label{sec:primer}

Readers who already know these metrics can skip to
Section~\ref{sec:formal}. All four return a badness score, higher is worse,
and curating by a metric means keeping the demonstrations with the lowest
scores. The intuition behind each metric comes first here, and the formal
definitions follow in Section~\ref{sec:formal}.

\textbf{SAL (Spectral Arc Length).} SAL looks only at how fast the end
effector is moving over time, ignoring where it goes. It takes that speed
trace, breaks it into its frequency content, and measures how long and
jagged the resulting spectrum is. A speed trace made of a few clean rises
and falls, as in a single deliberate reach, has a spectrum that decays
quickly and smoothly, which gives a short arc and a good score. A trace full
of small accelerations and hesitations has content at many frequencies and
a longer arc. The metric was developed in rehabilitation science to score
the reaching movements of patients against those of healthy subjects, and
the argument for applying it to demonstrations is that a skilled operator
moves in a few committed strokes while an unskilled one makes many small
adjustments.

\textbf{TED (Trajectory-Envelope Distance).} TED asks how far the recorded
motion strays from a smoothed copy of itself. It cuts the episode into
pieces at every moment the gripper opens or closes, fits each piece with a
smooth curve (the envelope), and reports the average gap between the
recorded pose and that curve along the best time alignment of the two.
Motion that a smooth curve can follow closely scores well. Motion with
excursions the curve cannot follow scores badly. The cuts at gripper events
are what make TED contact-aware: the abrupt change of motion that
necessarily accompanies a grasp or a release falls on a segment boundary
instead of being counted as a deviation, so what remains is meant to be
the operator's wobble within each phase of the task.

\textbf{Jerk.} Jerk is the rate at which acceleration changes. Mean jerk
takes the third difference of the end-effector position at every timestep
and averages its magnitude over the episode. Motion that speeds up and
slows down gradually scores well. Abrupt starts, stops, and changes of
direction score badly. It is the classical smoothness measure in the
motor-control literature and the crude baseline that SAL and TED were
proposed to improve on. The usual criticisms are that it amplifies sensor
noise and that it does not distinguish a deliberate fast motion from a
shaky one.

\textbf{Path length.} Path length is the total distance the end effector
travels. A demonstration that goes more or less directly from start to goal
scores well. One that wanders, backtracks, or makes repeated attempts
scores badly. The intuition is that a skilled operator takes the direct
route.

\textbf{Which of these grow with episode length.} Two of the four are tied
to duration and two are not. SAL's arc is a sum over the $T/2$ frequency
bins of the spectrum, and on real data every bin carries some noise and
adds to the sum, so recording the same motion for twice as long raises SAL
even when nothing about the execution has changed. This is a property of
the formula \rinse{} publishes; the standard definition normalizes it away
(Section~\ref{sec:formal}). On can/mh and square/mh,
SAL's correlation with $T$ is above $+0.95$ (Section~\ref{sec:detection}).
Path length grows with distance rather than time, so a slow retrace of the
same route scores the same as a fast one, but an operator who takes longer
usually also travels further, and its correlation with $T$ is $+0.90$ on
square/mh (and $+0.86$ on the secondary can/paired split). Jerk and TED are averages per
timestep (TED per step of the time alignment) and do not accumulate with
$T$. TED's correlation with $T$ on can/mh is $+0.14$. Slowing the same
motion down lowers every acceleration along it, so jerk, if anything,
favors slower execution of the same path.

Figure~\ref{fig:primer} shows the two behaviors that the rest of the paper
turns on, using three synthetic speed profiles scored with our own
implementations. Demonstration (b) is demonstration (a) executed twice as
slowly, with every segment exactly as smooth, and SAL rates it the worst of
the three, by a wider margin than it charges the committed correction in
(c). SAL rewards shortness. TED rates the correction in (c) as the worst of
the three and the slow copy in (b) as the best. TED penalizes committed
motion. On real data, the first behavior appears as SAL tracking episode
length almost exactly (Section~\ref{sec:detection}), and the second as TED
rating the low-deviation hovering of failed can/mg demonstrations as
cleaner than the committed contact-phase motion of successful ones
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Formal definitions, datasets, and statistics}
\label{sec:formal}

\textbf{Metrics.} All metrics are computed from proprioceptive signals at the
robomimic control rate of 20\,Hz ($\Delta t = 0.05$\,s) and return a badness
score (higher is worse). \emph{SAL}: spectral arc length of the end-effector
speed profile, implemented from the published formula; we negate so that
higher means less smooth. \rinse{}'s formula takes the arc length of the
unnormalized log-magnitude spectrum over every frequency up to Nyquist. It
differs from the standard SPARC definition~\citep{sparc}, which normalizes
the magnitude spectrum by its peak, rescales the frequency axis to $[0,1]$,
and truncates the spectrum at a cutoff frequency and an amplitude threshold,
so that the arc length does not grow with the number of spectral bins.
\emph{SPARC}: the standard definition with its usual settings (cutoff
10\,Hz, which is the Nyquist frequency at this control rate; amplitude
threshold 0.05; zero-padding level 4), reported alongside \rinse{}'s SAL
wherever the distinction matters. \emph{TED}: contact-aware trajectory-envelope
distance, reimplemented from the \rinse{} appendix: the 6-D pose trajectory
(position plus rotation vector, orientation weight $w_{\mathrm{ori}} =
0.25$) is partitioned at gripper open/close transitions, each segment is fit
with a greedy B\'ezier envelope, and the score is the length-normalized DTW
distance between trajectory and envelope. \emph{Jerk}: mean third-difference
magnitude of end-effector position. \emph{Path length}: total end-effector
arc length. Where indicated we also report per-timestep normalizations
(score divided by $T{-}1$). Our TED is a reimplementation from the paper, not
the authors' code; Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses the resulting
caveat and the sanity check we ran.

\textbf{Datasets.} All data is public robomimic~\citep{robomimic} low-dim.
\emph{can/mh}: 300 successful demonstrations from six human operators
with released skill tiers (better/okay/worse, 100 each, two operators per tier); episode lengths
range 98--1050 steps. All skill AUROCs compare better against worse; the
okay tier is excluded. \emph{can/mg}: 3900 machine-generated demonstrations
(300 rollouts from each of 13 checkpoints of a SAC training run), 18.4\%
successful, every episode exactly 150 steps. \emph{can/paired}: 200
demonstrations in matched success/failure pairs (50\% successful), lengths
55--147; we report it as a secondary setting because its matched-pair
construction makes it adversarially hard for trajectory-level scores.
\emph{square/mh}: 300 successful demonstrations with the same skill-tier
structure (100 each), episode lengths 123--1051, used in
Section~\ref{sec:square} to test whether the length confound is
task-specific. We note that robomimic ships machine-generated (\texttt{mg}) splits for Can
and Lift only, and a \texttt{paired} split for Can only; Square, Transport,
and Tool Hang ship only proficient-human and multi-human data, all of it
successful. \emph{lift/mg}: 1500 machine-generated demonstrations (300 rollouts from
each of 5 SAC checkpoints), 16.2\% successful at the final step, every
episode exactly 150 steps (Section~\ref{sec:lift}). The lift/mg scores and
all SPARC scores were computed from the v1.5 release of the files; episode
lengths and success labels are identical to the v1.4.1 files used for the
other scores, and recomputed SAL and jerk agree to rounding error.

\textbf{Statistics.} All AUROCs are reported with 95\% bootstrap confidence
intervals (2000 resamples). Downstream comparisons use difference-of-means
bootstrap CIs (20{,}000 resamples) and Welch two-sample tests, with
Bonferroni correction applied within each architecture's grid (8 BC tests,
4 diffusion tests; pooling all 12 changes no conclusion); we mark which
results survive correction and which do not. The band CIs in
Tables~\ref{tab:bands} and~\ref{tab:square} come from our original band
analysis; the reanalysis scripts that produce the operator-level and
SPARC numbers use a different bootstrap seed, so their intervals for the
same cells differ in the second or third decimal. Two AUROCs
computed on the same demonstrations are compared with the paired DeLong
test. For skill we report better-vs-worse AUROC; for
success we report AUROC against the binary success label with scores negated
so that AUROC $>$ 0.5 means the metric ranks clean/successful demonstrations
higher, and AUROC $<$ 0.5 means the metric is \emph{inverted}.

\subsection{Operator skill on can/mh: the length confound, on real labels}

On the full 200 better/worse demonstrations, SAL appears excellent: AUROC
0.952 [0.922, 0.975]. But episode length alone achieves 0.957 [0.929,
0.980], and SAL's correlation with $T$ is $+0.97$. The tier means make the
mechanism plain: worse operators average 304 steps, better operators 143,
and SAL's tier means (100.4 / 57.5 / 44.9) track duration almost linearly.
On this dataset SAL is, to first order, a stopwatch.

Two independent controls confirm this. First, \emph{normalization}: SAL's
arc length sums over $T/2$ spectral bins, so dividing by $T{-}1$ is the
principled per-timestep correction; normalized SAL drops from 0.952 to 0.582
[0.497, 0.662] and its length correlation drops from $+0.97$ to $+0.07$.
Second, \emph{duration matching}: restricting to bands of increasingly
similar episode length (Figure~\ref{fig:main}a, Table~\ref{tab:bands}). In
the tightest band ($T \in [140,175]$, $n{=}40$), where length itself is at
chance (0.485 [0.227, 0.740]), SAL is 0.628 [0.378, 0.856], a CI that
includes chance, while raw jerk is 0.896 [0.783, 0.980]. As the confound
is removed, SAL weakens at every step (0.952, 0.793, 0.759, 0.628) while
jerk strengthens and then levels off (0.584, 0.862, 0.906, 0.896). Only
SAL's side of this pattern is part of our claim: Section~\ref{sec:operators}
shows that it is robust to operator composition, whereas jerk's rise is
produced by which operators each band retains.

TED is near chance in most bands (0.558 full-sample); its one interval that
excludes chance is 0.653 [0.522, 0.776] in the widest band, and in the
tightest band it falls to 0.359 with an interval that spans chance. TED
does \emph{not} have the length pathology (correlation $+0.14$ with $T$);
it simply carries little skill signal on this data.

We caution that the tightest band retains only 7 worse-operator
demonstrations and its intervals are wide; the claim about SAL rests on its
trend across all four bands and its agreement with the normalization
control, not on any single cell. We also note that na\"ive per-timestep
normalization is only appropriate for metrics whose raw value grows with
$T$; normalized path length, for example, becomes an inverse-speed proxy
that \emph{rises} with skill and is reported only for completeness in
Table~\ref{tab:bands}.

\subsection{Task success on can/mg: the confound removed by construction}
\label{sec:mg}

The can/mg split fixes every episode at 150 steps, so no metric can exploit
duration. Against the success label (716 of 3900 successful), jerk achieves
0.706 [0.689, 0.724], SAL 0.454 [0.432, 0.477], path length 0.421 [0.402,
0.440], and TED 0.376 [0.355, 0.396] (Figure~\ref{fig:main}b). Those
intervals resample rollouts. The split is 13 checkpoints of 300 rollouts
whose success rate rises from 0\% to 68\%, so rollouts are not independent;
resampling whole checkpoints gives jerk [0.573, 0.832], SAL [0.392, 0.527],
path length [0.284, 0.563], and TED [0.300, 0.447]. The length baseline is exactly 0.500, as it must
be. Per-timestep normalization leaves every AUROC unchanged, as expected
under a constant divisor, which we report as a consistency check.

Both of \rinse{}'s metrics are at or below chance on this split. Three
qualifications change how that should be read.

First, the comparison is confounded with checkpoint. The first six
checkpoints contain no successes at all, so a metric can score well or badly
against success simply through how it rates early-training rollouts.
Restricting to the six checkpoints with at least 20 rollouts of each
outcome, and weighting each by its number of success/failure pairs, the
within-checkpoint AUROCs are jerk 0.823, SAL 0.526, path length 0.490, and
TED 0.484. Among rollouts of the same policy TED is at chance. Its pooled
inversion arises because it gives its best scores to the second checkpoint
(mean 0.020, against 0.049--0.095 for the others), which never succeeds.
Filtering by TED does preferentially retain failed demonstrations in this
pool, but it does so through checkpoint identity, not through a
within-policy preference for failures. Jerk's signal, by contrast, is
stronger within checkpoints than pooled.

Second, SAL's weakness is specific to \rinse{}'s formula. Standard SPARC
reaches 0.871 on the same rollouts ([0.858, 0.882] resampling rollouts,
[0.820, 0.908] resampling checkpoints) and 0.854 within checkpoints.

Third, TED's result does not replicate on the second machine-generated
split, which we turn to next.

\subsection{A second success-label task: lift/mg}
\label{sec:lift}

robomimic's other machine-generated split has the same construction: 1500
rollouts from five SAC checkpoints, each exactly 150 steps, of which 243
(16.2\%) end in success. Only the last two checkpoints contain successes
(17\% and 64\%). Against the success label, standard SPARC reaches 0.891
[0.861, 0.919], jerk 0.870 [0.843, 0.894], TED 0.669 [0.639, 0.700],
\rinse{}'s SAL 0.643 [0.607, 0.679], and path length 0.431 [0.401, 0.462].
Within the two checkpoints that have successes, the AUROCs are SPARC 0.739
and 0.863, jerk 0.806 and 0.862, TED 0.612 and 0.740, SAL 0.537 and 0.691,
and path length 0.245 and 0.597. Scoring success as any rewarded step (316
rollouts) instead of the final step changes no ordering. With five
checkpoints a checkpoint-level bootstrap is uninformative and we do not
report one.

Two things carry over from Can and one does not. Jerk and standard SPARC
separate successes from failures on both tasks, pooled and within
checkpoint, and \rinse{}'s SAL is the weaker of the two spectral measures on
both. TED is not inverted on Lift. The statement that TED cannot tell
failures from successes therefore holds for can/mg and not for
machine-generated data in general. We have not run downstream training on
Lift.

\subsection{Secondary setting: can/paired}

On the matched-pair dataset every metric is inverted against success (TED
0.331, SAL 0.398, jerk 0.368, path length 0.029), but the setting is
confounded in the opposite direction: successful demonstrations are
systematically \emph{longer} (length-alone AUROC 0.194), and path length's
$+0.86$ correlation with $T$ makes it a pure length detector here. We
report can/paired for completeness and treat can/mg as the primary
success-label evidence.

\subsection{Generalization to a second task: square/mh}
\label{sec:square}

If the length confound were an artifact of the Can task, the argument would
be considerably weaker. We therefore repeat the skill-label analysis on
square/mh, a harder insertion task with the same operator skill-tier
structure and a comparable duration range (123--1051 steps versus Can's
98--1050). No retuning was performed: the same metric implementations and
the same band procedure were applied to the new data.

The confound replicates almost exactly. SAL's correlation with episode
duration is $+0.96$ on Square against $+0.97$ on Can, and its uncontrolled
skill AUROC is 0.898 [0.852, 0.936] against a length-alone baseline of 0.936
[0.902, 0.964]. Per-timestep normalization collapses it to 0.458 [0.376,
0.536], slightly below chance and a larger drop than the corresponding Can
figure. Path length behaves the same way, reaching 0.931 [0.893, 0.963]
uncontrolled with a $+0.90$ duration correlation.

SAL's loss of signal also replicates (Table~\ref{tab:square}). As the
duration band tightens and the length baseline falls from 0.936 to 0.407,
SAL falls at every step (0.898, 0.759, 0.529, 0.480), to a CI centered on
chance in the band where duration is genuinely controlled ($T \in
[200,240]$, length AUROC 0.407): 0.480 [0.250, 0.701]. Jerk rises over the
same bands (0.431, 0.570, 0.782, 0.896) and reaches the same 0.896 it
reaches in Can's tightest band, but as on Can that value depends on which
operators the band retains (Section~\ref{sec:operators}).

Two differences from Can are worth stating. First, jerk is \emph{inverted}
on Square before any control is applied (0.431), where on Can it was weakly
positive (0.584); the uncontrolled ranking of metrics is therefore itself
task-dependent, even though SAL's loss of its advantage under the control
is not. Second, path
length remains strong in the controlled band on Square (0.887 [0.742,
0.991]) where it collapsed on Can (0.429 [0.215, 0.645]). We do not claim
jerk or path length is a good curation metric on the strength of these
numbers; the point is narrower, that whatever advantage SAL displays over
them disappears once duration is held fixed, on both tasks. The tightest
Square band retains 13 worse-operator demonstrations, 11 of them from one
operator, so as with Can the claim about SAL rests on its trend across
bands rather than on any single cell.

\section{Does Detection Predict Curation? Downstream Training}
\label{sec:training}

Detection AUROC is not the quantity practitioners care
about~\citep{bedi-policy}; the trained policy is. We therefore filter can/mg
by each metric at a 50\% keep fraction and train a GMM behavior-cloning
policy (MLP $1024{\times}1024$, 5 modes, batch 100, 600 epochs of 500
gradient steps, learning rate $10^{-4}$) on each curated subset, with 50
evaluation rollouts (horizon 400) at eight points per run (epochs 100, 150,
200, 300, 400, 450, 500, and 600: every 150 epochs plus each 100-epoch
checkpoint save) and the best of the eight reported per seed. Conditions: TED, SAL, jerk, path length, plus three
references: an \emph{oracle} keeping only successful demonstrations, a
\emph{random} 50\% subset, and a second, independent random 50\% subset that
our tables label ``no curation''. That label is a misnomer kept for
continuity with the released results: it is a size-matched random draw
(sharing 979 demonstrations with the first), not the full
3900-demonstration pool. Training on the full pool and on a pool curated by
standard SPARC was added in this version (Section~\ref{sec:fullpool}). Ten
seeds per condition. All filtered pools contain 1950 demonstrations except the
oracle (716, the number of successes that exist); we therefore treat the
oracle as a ceiling reference rather than a matched condition.

\subsection{Curating can/mh: \rinse{}'s SAL against episode length alone}
\label{sec:mhtrain}

On can/mh, every demonstration succeeds, and SAL's detection signal is
almost entirely episode length (Section~\ref{sec:detection}). That
predicts, for curation, that keeping the demonstrations SAL rates
smoothest should do about as well as keeping the shortest ones. We test this
directly: GMM-BC in the same configuration, trained on the top 150 of the
300 can/mh demonstrations ranked by SAL, by episode length alone, and on a
random 150, ten seeds each, evaluated in the Can environment exactly as
above.

Both rankings select largely the same demonstrations (133 of 150 in
common) and largely the better operators: the SAL half contains 84
better-tier and 8 worse-tier demonstrations, the length half 89 and 9, and
the random half 47 and 55. Both curated pools train far better than random:
SAL 0.854 $\pm$ 0.044 and length 0.878 $\pm$ 0.036 against random 0.650
$\pm$ 0.064 ($+0.204$ and $+0.228$, both $p < 10^{-6}$ and surviving
correction across the three tests). SAL and length are not distinguishable
($-0.024$, CI $[-0.056, +0.010]$, $p = 0.20$; under the
mean-over-evaluations estimator $-0.026$, $p = 0.058$), and the point
estimate favors length.

This result cuts in two directions, and we report both. It shows that
curating can/mh by \rinse{}'s SAL does improve a policy over random
selection of the same size, so on human data of mixed skill SAL is a useful
filter. It also shows that the improvement is reproduced, and not
exceeded, by the episode-length baseline that needs no trajectory analysis
at all, which is what the length confound predicts. A curation gain
reported for SAL on data of this kind is therefore a gain that length alone
would have delivered.

\subsection{Cross-architecture replication under a diffusion policy}
\label{sec:crossarch}

The grid above uses a single policy configuration, so on its own it cannot
distinguish a property of the metric from a property of behavior cloning. We
therefore replicated it under a diffusion policy on the same curated
subsets: same keep fraction, same 1950-demonstration pools, same 600 epochs
of 500 gradient steps, same learning rate ($10^{-4}$), and the same
evaluation protocol (50 rollouts, horizon 400, the same eight evaluation epochs, best of eight per seed).\footnote{Reporting the best checkpoint per seed is a
maximum over the eight evaluations each run receives, and is therefore
biased upward; both grids are evaluated eight times, so the bias is
symmetric across architectures. Recomputing every comparison with the
\emph{mean} over the eight evaluations instead leaves all conclusions
intact, with one exception we record here: TED versus no curation under
diffusion reaches $p = 0.0110$ under the mean estimator and would survive
this architecture's correction threshold ($\alpha = 0.0125$), whereas under
the reported best-of-eight estimator it does not ($p = 0.11$). TED versus
random remains non-significant under both ($p = 0.12$ and $p = 0.17$). We
report the conservative version throughout.} The policy is a conditional
U-Net ($[256, 512, 1024]$
down-dims, kernel 5, 8 groups, 256-dimensional diffusion-step embedding)
with EMA (power 0.75), an observation horizon of 2, action horizon of 8, and
prediction horizon of 16, using a DDIM noise scheduler with 10 inference
steps. Batch size (256 vs.\ 100) and the sequence modeling are inherent to
the architecture and are the only differences between the two grids beyond
the policy architecture itself. We ran five conditions (TED, jerk, and the three
references) with five seeds each; SAL and path length were not rerun, so the
diffusion comparison covers two of the four metrics.

Three results follow, and they do not all point the same way.

First, TED is the worst condition under both architectures (0.240 under BC,
0.092 under diffusion). That is what one expects if the deficit belongs to
the metric rather than to behavior cloning, and the replication is
consistent with that part of the original finding. But the harm is statistically
robust under BC only. Under diffusion the deficit is $-0.024$ against random
and $-0.028$ against no curation, with intervals that admit zero, and
neither survives correction. This is a statement about power rather than a
refutation: at five seeds this grid resolves differences of roughly 0.054,
essentially the same threshold we report for the BC grid, and TED's deficit
falls below it. The defensible claim is that TED's \emph{ordering} holds
across architectures while its \emph{harm} is demonstrated under BC only.
Descriptively, and not as a tested comparison, the deficit is of broadly
similar relative size in the two grids: 37\% of the no-curation baseline
under BC and 23\% under diffusion. We report this to characterize how the
compressed diffusion success scale relates to the absolute differences in
Table~\ref{tab:crossarch_tests}, not as evidence that the two effects are
equal.

Second, jerk reverses sign. Under BC it is indistinguishable from both
baselines ($p \geq 0.06$); under diffusion it beats both by $+0.096$ and
$+0.092$, and both comparisons survive correction. This is the largest
effect in the diffusion grid, and it runs opposite to the behavior-cloning
grid: a crude trajectory statistic, never proposed as a curation metric,
shows curation value in the configuration we tested. The jerk
pool also contains the most successes of any metric-curated pool in the
diffusion grid (Table~\ref{tab:pools}), so the open question is less why it helps under
diffusion than why it does not under behavior cloning. With five seeds at
success rates near 0.1--0.2, we treat the reversal as provisional. What may
drive it is taken up below.

Third, absolute success rates are lower under diffusion in every condition,
by factors ranging from $1.6\times$ on the oracle (0.544 vs.\ 0.892) and on
jerk (0.212 vs.\ 0.332) to $3.0$--$3.2\times$ on the two baselines, at a
matched epoch and evaluation budget in which the diffusion runs in fact saw
more samples per epoch (batch 256 vs.\ 100). The gap is therefore not a
uniform offset: it is widest on the two random pools and narrowest on the
two pools with the highest share of successes.

One explanation can be ruled out. The diffusion runs show no measurable
improvement over the second half of training: averaged across all 25 runs,
mean success in the last four evaluations exceeds the first four by
$+0.017$, against a binomial noise floor of $0.044$ for 50-rollout
evaluations, and only 3 of 25 runs improve by more than one noise standard
deviation. Per-run variation across the eight evaluations has a standard
deviation $1.01\times$ the noise floor, which is to say it is
indistinguishable from sampling noise alone. The identical measurement on
the BC grid gives $+0.092$, with 56 of 70 runs improving and a ratio of
$1.46\times$, so the flatness is a property of the diffusion runs and not
of our evaluation protocol. Whatever limits these policies is therefore not
training duration; they converge early and stop. We record this because
under-training is the most natural explanation for the gap and it does not
survive contact with the training curves, which narrows the space of
remaining explanations without confirming one. One candidate is action
parameterization: our runs used robomimic's default delta actions, which
Chi et al.~\citep{chi-dp} report handicap diffusion policy while helping BC,
so the gap and the jerk reversal may partly reflect it, and reading either
as a property of the policy configuration (architecture and action
parameterization) is provisional. The consequence for reading
Table~\ref{tab:crossarch} is concrete: the two
columns are not operating at comparable points on the success scale, so
cross-architecture comparisons of absolute differences are weaker than
within-column comparisons, and the claims above are deliberately about
ordering and sign rather than magnitude.

We correct within each architecture, since each grid is a separate family of
comparisons. Pooling all twelve tests instead ($\alpha = 0.05/12 = 0.00417$)
changes nothing: TED's two BC comparisons and jerk's two diffusion
comparisons still survive, and path length's $p = 0.0071$ still does not.

Taken together, the replication is consistent with the metric-level reading of the TED
result and simultaneously narrows the scope of any claim about smoothness
metrics as a class. Which demonstrations are worth keeping is not a property
of the demonstrations alone, and a curation result reported without naming
the policy configuration it was measured on, architecture and action
parameterization included, omits something that can change the sign of the
answer.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_qual_pair.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Failure stays inside the envelope; success does not.} A successful (left)
and failed (right) can/mg demonstration, with end-effector position, TED's
gripper-contact segment boundaries (red), the per-segment B\'ezier envelope
(dashed), and the per-timestep envelope residual (bottom, orange bars are
per-segment means). Both trajectories contain one high-deviation segment,
but in the success it spans timesteps 12--96 and coincides with the
descend-and-grasp, while in the failure it is confined to the initial
free-space approach (0--24) and the remaining 126 steps are a flat tail of
repeated gripper cycles that never completes a placement. TED averages over
the alignment path and so rates the failure as the cleaner trajectory.
Illustrative of the mechanism only; the quantitative claim is
Figure~\ref{fig:main}b.}
\label{fig:qual}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Skill and success are different targets.} \rinse{}'s SAL gives
different verdicts depending on the label. Under an uncontrolled skill
label it looks nearly perfect; under a length-controlled skill label it is
marginal; under a success label on fixed-length data it is weak on both
machine-generated tasks. Curation papers validate against operator skill or
smoothness-adjacent labels on success-only pools, then are deployed, in
practice, to remove \emph{failures} from mixed pools. Those are not the same
job, and SAL as \rinse{} defines it does not transfer between them. Standard
SPARC is less length-dependent, much stronger against success, and against
skill better than \rinse{}'s SAL once duration is controlled on Square
(though not on Can), so the failure against success is a statement about a
formula and not about spectral smoothness.

\textbf{What we no longer claim about jerk.} An earlier version of this
paper said that the crude baseline wins under every length control. Against
task success that remains true on both machine-generated tasks, pooled and
within checkpoints: jerk outranks both of \rinse{}'s metrics. Against
operator skill it does not hold in the form we stated. Jerk's high AUROC in
the duration-matched bands is one operator pair, and across operator pairs
it is more often below chance than above (Section~\ref{sec:operators}). Nor do
we claim jerk is a good curation metric: its can/mg pool had two-thirds more
successes than a random one, produced no gain over random selection under
behavior cloning, and trained worse than the full pool.

\textbf{Why TED is below chance on can/mg.} The quantitative account is the
checkpoint one of Section~\ref{sec:mg}: TED rates the rollouts of an early,
never-successful checkpoint as the smoothest in the pool, and within a
checkpoint it does not separate outcomes. The qualitative account that
follows is consistent with that and should be read with it in mind. The two
demonstrations in Figure~\ref{fig:qual} come from different checkpoints (the
failure from the third, the success from the seventh, whose success rate
is 1\%), so the figure
illustrates a contrast between policies at different stages of training,
which is where the inversion lives, and not between two outcomes of one
policy. TED's design premise is that
partitioning at gripper-contact boundaries isolates task-relevant
abruptness, and a plausible hypothesis going in was that this would let TED
catch failures, since canonical structural failures are contact-boundary
events. The data are consistent with a different and simpler mechanism.
Figure~\ref{fig:qual} contrasts a successful and a failed can/mg
demonstration. Both contain one high-deviation segment, but they differ in
when it occurs and how much of the episode it spans. In the success, the
high-residual segment runs from timestep 12 to 96 (84 of 150 steps, mean
residual 0.259) and coincides with the descend-and-grasp: the end effector
dips, the lateral axes swing, and the envelope cannot track the motion. In
the failure, the only comparable deviation is the initial free-space
approach (timesteps 0--24, mean residual 0.213); after that the trajectory
settles into a flat tail in which the end effector holds height while the
gripper opens and closes repeatedly without completing a placement, and
every remaining segment has residual below 0.014. Because TED averages
deviation over the DTW alignment path, a committed motion spanning half the
episode raises the score substantially while a short approach transient is
diluted by the smooth remainder. TED therefore rates the failure as the
cleaner trajectory (0.042 vs.\ 0.144).

On can/mg, in other words, the later policies that sometimes succeed
produce sustained high-deviation motion through the contact phase, while
early policies produce low-amplitude hovering that the envelope follows
closely. On lift/mg the same metric is above chance
(Section~\ref{sec:lift}), so this is a description of one split. A
metric that rewards agreement with a smoothed envelope is measuring
commitment with the sign flipped. Within a policy that sometimes succeeds,
jerk sees the same two behaviors from the other side, which may be why it
and standard SPARC retain signal within checkpoints. A committed motion is
large but smooth: it carries the end effector far from the envelope, but
its accelerations change gradually, so its envelope deviation is high and
its jerk is low. The failed hovering of such a policy is small but jittery:
the end effector holds position through repeated gripper cycles, and what
little it moves, it moves abruptly, so its envelope deviation is low and
its jerk is high. This contrast is within-policy. Across checkpoints it does
not hold in general: the fourth and fifth checkpoints never succeed and are
the lowest-jerk rollouts in the split (Section~\ref{sec:training}). The two
demonstrations in Figure~\ref{fig:qual} show both readings at once, with
the success at a mean jerk of 71.6 against 124.1 for the failure while TED
rates the failure cleaner. Across the whole split successful demonstrations
have lower mean jerk than failed ones (85.7 vs.\ 112.8), the direction the
0.706 AUROC reports, and within the last four checkpoints the gap is 85.6
vs.\ 125.5.
We stress that these panels illustrate a mechanism rather than
establish it; the quantitative claims are the AUROCs of 0.376 and 0.706 in
Figure~\ref{fig:main}b, and the per-demonstration TED scores we inspected
(successes 0.144 and 0.079, failures 0.042, 0.042, and 0.017) are
consistent with but not evidence for those numbers.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Our skill-label results cover two robomimic tasks (Can and Square) with two
operators per tier, which is too few to separate a metric's relation to
skill from its relation to individual operators; the jerk result of
Section~\ref{sec:operators} is the case where that mattered, and the
duration-matched bands reduce to a single operator pair on both tasks. Our
success-label results cover the two machine-generated splits robomimic
ships (Can and Lift), in which outcome is confounded with policy checkpoint;
Lift has successes in only two of its five checkpoints. Downstream training
covers Can only (can/mg, and can/mh for SAL and length); we have not trained
on Lift or on Square. The full-pool and SPARC runs were made in a rebuilt
environment and are compared with the original runs; five re-run seeds
reproduce the originals exactly, but the cross-grid comparisons rest on that
check. The can/mh experiment has no full-pool arm, so it shows that SAL
curation matches length curation, not how either compares with training on
all 300 demonstrations. Extending the
success-label analysis beyond policy rollouts requires datasets with real, labeled teleoperation
failures, such as \mbox{REASSEMBLE}~\citep{reassemble} or
DROID~\citep{droid}. Our downstream results cover two policy configurations (GMM behavior
cloning and a diffusion policy); the detection results are policy-free. The
cross-architecture grid of Section~\ref{sec:crossarch} carries three
limitations of its own. It covers two of the four metrics, since SAL and
path length were not rerun under diffusion, so the claim that TED ranks
worst under both architectures rests on a five-condition subset on the
diffusion side against a seven-condition grid on the BC side. It uses five
seeds rather than ten, resolving differences of roughly 0.054; TED's
diffusion deficit ($-0.028$, CI $[-0.052, +0.000]$) falls below that, which
is why we report its ordering as replicating and its harm as established
under BC only. And absolute success rates remain lower under diffusion in
every condition (Section~\ref{sec:crossarch}); the training curves rule out
under-training as the cause, and action parameterization is a candidate we
have not yet tested, so we restrict cross-architecture claims to ordering
and sign rather than magnitude.
Our TED is a reimplementation from the \rinse{} appendix, not the authors'
released code; a discrepancy with the reference implementation cannot be
excluded, and we will replace it if code becomes available. Can's tightest
duration band retains only 7 negatives, all from one operator (Square's
retains 13, 11 from one operator), and its role in our argument is
corroborative, not primary. The BC downstream
conditions use 10 seeds; at this power, differences smaller than roughly
0.05 in mean success rate are not resolvable, which is why we report SAL and
jerk as indistinguishable from the baselines rather than as harmful under
that architecture. The oracle training
condition has a smaller pool (716 vs.\ 1950) and is reported as a ceiling,
not a matched comparison. Finally, our success-label experiments use
machine-generated failures (can/mg, lift/mg); failure modes of human teleoperators
may present differently. We report can/paired only as a secondary setting
because its matched-pair construction, in which each success/failure pair
shares a near-identical trajectory prefix and diverges only in the final
placement, concentrates the discriminating signal into a handful of
terminal timesteps and simultaneously introduces an opposing length
confound (successful demonstrations are systematically longer, length-alone
AUROC 0.194); neither property is representative of the mixed-quality pools
curation metrics are deployed on.

\section{Conclusion}

Evaluated where their authors did not evaluate them, on real operator
labels under duration controls and on fixed-length data against task
success, \rinse{}'s two metrics do not behave as curation scores should.
Its SAL ranks episode duration: it matches the length baseline overall and
within every operator pair, on two tasks, and is weak against task success
on both fixed-length splits. That is largely a property of the published
formula, since the standard SPARC definition is less length-dependent and
separates successes from failures well on both splits. Its TED is below
chance against success on can/mg, where it favors the rollouts of an early
checkpoint, at chance within checkpoints, and above chance on lift/mg.
Curating can/mg by TED costs a third of downstream behavior-cloning success
against random selection of the same size, a result robust to correction
for multiple comparisons and matched by the composition of the pool TED
selects; the other three metrics we trained on are not distinguishable from
random subsets. Against training on the full pool, all four metrics lose,
TED by 0.166, while a pool curated by standard SPARC wins (0.480 against
0.406). On can/mh, curating by SAL beats random selection but not the
episode-length baseline, so its curation gain there is the length gain. An
oracle reaches 0.892 on the can/mg pool.

Two things we reported earlier are weaker than we said. Raw jerk's advantage
under duration matching is one pair of operators and does not generalize
across the operators in the data. And the diffusion-policy replication, in
which TED still ranks worst without a resolvable deficit and jerk moves
above both baselines, rests on five seeds at low success rates in one action
parameterization, and is provisional. What remains open is why a jerk-curated
pool with two-thirds more successes than a random one trains no better under
behavior cloning, when a SPARC-curated pool with a similar surplus trains
clearly better.
